> *“The mind is not a vessel to be filled, but a fire to be kindled.”*  
> — after Plutarch

## 1. The question that made them laugh

There is a memory that has never really left me, one of those tiny memories that, when you look back at them across the years, turn out to be keys, little hidden doors through which you suddenly understand a whole system, and this memory is of an ordinary classroom, an ordinary teacher, an ordinary lesson, and of me, sitting somewhere between the radiator and the window, no longer listening. Not because I was bored, not because I looked down on what was being said, but because what was being said had just sent me somewhere else, like a stone thrown into water whose ripples keep widening long after the stone has vanished, and I was following those ripples, pulling on the threads of what I was beginning to understand, drifting off into my own world, yes, but drifting off into my own world in order to understand the subject, to turn it over, to see what lay behind it, beside it, beneath it.

And then I would come back. I would come back with a question, a real question, one of those that seemed obvious to me, relevant, even necessary, because it was the culmination of the whole inner journey I had just made, and I would raise my hand, which I did very, very rarely, and I would ask it, convinced I had understood something. And the teacher didn’t understand. He didn’t understand the direction, he couldn’t see where the question was coming from or where it was trying to go, because he hadn’t made the journey with me, and the class laughed, not to mock me, but because the question was disconcerting, because it came out of nowhere, because you didn’t often hear that kind of question in a classroom, and laughter is often a group’s first reaction to whatever surprises it. And I found it funny too. I wasn’t hurt, I wasn’t humiliated, I was even rather pleased with myself, because I could feel that I had touched something, that my question unsettled people, that it simply wasn’t the one they were expecting.

I had no answer, and I simply told myself: all right, fine, maybe I see things differently, and there is no answer to that difference here, not in this setting, not now. And I moved on, without drama, keeping my threads to myself. I hold no grudge against anyone, not against that teacher, who was doing his job with thirty pupils in front of him and a curriculum to get through, nor against my classmates, whom the question had simply caught off guard. But looking back, what strikes me is not the laughter, it is the silence that followed: a real question went nowhere, not because it was a bad one, but because the setting had no room for it. And I wonder how many children have lived through the same thing, less serenely than I did perhaps, in how many classrooms, for how many generations, and how many ended up keeping their questions to themselves, for want of finding someone to follow them?

It is from this memory that I want to begin, because I believe it holds the whole question of education in the age of artificial intelligence, and because I believe that for the first time in our history we hold in our hands a tool capable of following children wherever they wander, at the speed of their thought, and of never letting a question go nowhere. The question is not whether this tool will enter school; it is already there, in pockets, in bedrooms, in the evening’s homework. The question is whether we will have the courage to let this tool transform school itself, or whether we will, once again, try to squeeze the new world into the boxes of the old.

## 2. The false debate: ban or allow

Ever since generative AI burst into our lives, the educational establishment seems to have been gripped by a defensive reflex that would be almost touching if it weren’t so revealing, and that reflex boils down to a single question, repeated in staff rooms, education authorities, ministries and television studios: how do we stop pupils from cheating? How do we detect homework written by a machine, how do we ban these tools during exams, how do we protect the essay, the text commentary, the maths exercise against this unfair competition that produces in ten seconds what a pupil used to spend three hours on? We buy detectors, we block websites, we go back to pen and paper, we organise supervised tests the way one would organise the defence of a besieged fortress.

I understand this fear, but I believe it is fighting the wrong battle, and fighting it in a way that says a great deal about what we really expect from school. Because if a machine can do in ten seconds the homework we ask of our children, the real question is not “how do we stop the machine from doing it,” but “why were we asking for that homework in the first place, and what was it supposed to build in the child?” If the purpose of an exercise was to produce a text that met an expectation, then yes, the machine has won, and it has won because we had already turned our children into little machines for producing texts that meet expectations. But if the purpose was to learn to think, to doubt, to connect, to create, then the machine threatens nothing at all: it merely forces us to look squarely at what we had lost along the way.

We won’t be able to ban the tool. We couldn’t ban the book, which religious authorities wanted to keep for the clergy, we couldn’t ban the calculator, which was supposed to kill mental arithmetic, we couldn’t ban the Internet, which was supposed to make pupils lazy and ignorant, and each time the technology ended up getting in, not because it had been allowed, but because it had become the world itself. Generative AI will follow the same path, only faster and deeper, because it doesn’t just touch access to information, it touches the very production of thought, writing, reasoning, creation. Trying to ban it from school means preparing our children for a world that will no longer exist by the time they leave.

So we have to change the question, and the question I want to ask is this one: now that this tool exists, now that it can converse, explain, rephrase, follow a line of reasoning, suggest paths, how can we use it to finally do what school has never been able to do, namely to accompany every child according to their own way of learning? This is no longer a question of discipline, it is a question of vision. And a vision is not something you detect with anti-cheating software; it is something you build.

## 3. One school, one tempo

To understand what AI could change, we first have to look honestly at what school, as we know it, actually is, not in order to condemn it, for it has achieved immense things, mass literacy, access to knowledge for millions of children who would otherwise have been deprived of it, the magnificent idea that education is a right and not a privilege, but in order to see clearly what model it was built on and what that model still produces today, almost in spite of itself. The modern school was born in the nineteenth century, in the same movement as the factory, and it has kept the factory’s deep grammar: homogeneous age groups, fixed timetables punctuated by a bell, an identical curriculum for all, cut up into watertight subjects, delivered by one adult facing a group, and assessed with marks that make it possible to rank, to sort, to steer.

This model had its logic, and even its grandeur, at a time when a whole nation had to be educated with few resources, when books were rare and teachers few, when the only way to pass knowledge on to everyone was to pass it on to everyone at the same time, in the same way, at the same pace. But what was a material constraint became, over time, a cultural given, almost a natural truth: we ended up believing that this was what learning meant, sitting down, listening, repeating, reciting back, and that the child who didn’t fit the mould had a problem, with attention, with behaviour, with motivation, rather than thinking that perhaps it was the mould that had a problem with the child.

A single tempo for thirty pupils is mathematically impossible for most of them to keep. For those who go faster, the lesson is one long tedium, an endless wait, a waiting room of knowledge where above all you learn to be patient and to pretend. For those who go more slowly, it is a race lost before it starts, where every notion not understood piles onto the one before until it forms a wall you end up believing is insurmountable, until you tell yourself you’re “hopeless at maths,” the way you’d say you have blue eyes, as if it were an identity and not a consequence. And for those who, like the child I was, wander off down side paths, it is a permanent misunderstanding, a way of thinking that never finds anyone to talk to.

And then there is what this model manufactures, slowly, patiently, year after year, in those who adapt to it best. It manufactures “school-minded” pupils, and I use that word without contempt, even with a kind of tenderness, because I still meet them today, as adults, in the training sessions I lead. These are people who learned that there is a right answer, that someone else holds it, and that the aim of the game is to find it and recite it back without a mistake. People who don’t dare stray from the path, who wait for instructions, who ask whether this is really what they were supposed to do. Is it their nature? I don’t believe so. I believe it is the trace, within them, of a system that rewarded conformity and punished deviation, a system that taught them, by leaving singular questions to go nowhere, that straying from the path is a risk.

## 4. There is no one way to learn

For several years now I have been training people in artificial intelligence and product design, adults from every background, technical profiles and creative profiles, people discovering the field and others already practising it, and if there is one thing I have learned from watching them learn, it is that there is no one way to learn, there are as many as there are people, and that the greatest mistake one can make, as a trainer or as an education system, is to believe that one of them is better than the others. When I put an AI into the hands of a group, I immediately see radically different paths taking shape, temperaments revealing themselves in the very way each person takes hold of the tool.

There are those who explore. They feel free, deeply free, and that freedom shows in their gestures: they try, they experiment, they ask a question, then another, then set off in an unexpected direction, pull on a thread, drop it to pull on another, go off in every direction, and out of this apparent disorder there often comes a dazzling understanding, an intuition that connects things nobody had thought to connect. These are the children who used to be called dreamy, distracted, scattered, the ones whose school reports said “could do better” or “must concentrate,” and who discover, in front of an AI, that they finally have someone to talk to who can follow their detours without ever telling them they are off topic.

And then there are those who analyse. They don’t throw themselves into exploration; they take the answer, and they look at it, for a long time, they pick it apart, they ask themselves why the machine says this, they come back to it, question it, check it, move forward step by step, building their understanding the way you build a house, stone by stone, foundation by foundation. You might think they are slower, and sometimes they are, but what they build is remarkably solid, and once they have understood, they have understood for good. These are not less free pupils; they are minds that need to understand before they dare, and that deserve to have this need respected instead of mistaken for a lack of boldness.

Between these two poles lie all the shades imaginable, and each of us moves along this spectrum depending on the subject, the moment, the inner state we find ourselves in. The same adult can be an explorer when faced with a subject that fascinates them and an analyst when faced with one that worries them. The same child may need to run in every direction on a Monday and dive into a single problem for the rest of the week. What I see, in short, is that learning is not a motorway on which everyone drives at different speeds; it is a landscape, with its forests, its rivers, its mountains, and each of us traces our own trail through it. No trail is superior to another. But a system that recognises only one condemns all the other walkers to feel lost.

## 5. What we had already dreamed

It would be dishonest to claim that this vision is new, and above all it would be an insult to all those who carried it before us, often alone, often against their own era, with a stubbornness that commands respect. At the beginning of the twentieth century, Maria Montessori, a doctor before she was an educator, observed children who were said to be incapable of learning and discovered that, on the contrary, they learned with an extraordinary thirst, as soon as they were offered a prepared environment and their pace was respected. The phrase attributed to her, “help me to do it myself,” contains a whole revolution on its own: the adult is no longer the one who does things for the child nor the one who dictates, but the one who makes autonomy possible.

A few years later, in a small school in the south of France, Célestin Freinet set up a printing press in his classroom and made the children the authors of their own texts, their own newspapers, their own correspondence with other schools. He started from their questions, their desires, their lives, in order to build their learning, and he had understood something the institution has taken a century to begin to hear: that a child working on a project that means something to them learns mathematics, grammar, history, not as abstract subjects, but as tools they need in order to achieve what they want to achieve.

In 1971, the thinker Ivan Illich went even further, with a book bearing a provocative title, *Deschooling Society*, in which he argued that the school institution confused teaching with learning, the diploma with competence, attendance at a place with the acquisition of knowledge, and he was already imagining “networks” that would allow everyone to find the resources, the peers and the mentors they needed to learn whatever they wanted to learn. There is, in these pages written more than half a century ago, an unsettling intuition of what today’s technologies make possible. At roughly the same time, in the United States, the Sudbury Valley School opened its doors on a dizzying principle: no imposed curriculum, the children choose what they learn, when and how, and the adults are there to accompany them. Much later, in 2006, Ken Robinson put to the whole world a question that would become one of the most watched talks in history: do schools kill creativity?

All these pioneers said, each in their own way, the same thing I am saying here. And yet their schools remained on the margins, islands in the ocean of standard schooling, often reserved for those who could pay for them or who were lucky enough to live in the right place. Why? Not because their ideas were wrong, the results were often remarkable, but because truly individual accompaniment is expensive, terribly expensive, in time, in people, in attention. One adult cannot follow with the same depth the paths of twenty-five different children, at the same time, all day long. The vision was right, but the economics of attention made it impossible to generalise.

## 6. What AI really changes

This is exactly where artificial intelligence changes the game, and this is where we need to be precise, because grand declarations about “AI that will revolutionise education” are often hollow, and the real change is both more modest and more profound than people say. AI does not invent a new pedagogy. It has no vision of the child, no philosophy of learning, no intuition about what a good life is. What it brings is something else: it makes possible, on an unprecedented scale, what was once reserved for the few, namely someone to talk to who is available, patient, infinitely adaptable, capable of following each child in their own direction, at their own pace, without ever tiring, without ever mocking, without ever telling them they are off topic.

Throughout history, tailor-made education has always existed, but it bore a name that says a lot: private tutoring. Alexander the Great had Aristotle. The children of royal and aristocratic families had tutors who lived with them, who followed their questions, who adapted every lesson to their temperament, their strengths, their weaknesses. It was the ultimate privilege, one only the most powerful could afford, and that is no accident: people knew, intuitively, that it was the best way to learn. In 1984, the researcher Benjamin Bloom measured it: pupils followed individually by a tutor achieved spectacularly better results than pupils in conventional group teaching, to the point that the average tutored pupil outperformed the vast majority of the traditional class. Bloom called this the “two sigma problem”: we knew how to do it, but we didn’t know how to do it for everyone.

Here is the heart of my argument, and it is a political argument before it is a pedagogical one: generative AI may be the first serious answer to Bloom’s problem. For the first time, the tutor of princes could become the tutor of every child, whether born in a well-off neighbourhood or in a forgotten suburb, in a big city or in a village where the nearest school is twenty kilometres away. This is not a technological promise, it is a promise of justice. Because today, personalised accompaniment already exists, it is called private lessons, tutoring, alternative private schools, and it is overwhelmingly reserved for families who can afford it. Used well, AI could break this monopoly on attention.

But be careful, because this is where everything is decided: AI does not replace the human tutor in what is essential about them. Aristotle was not merely an encyclopaedia at Alexander’s disposal; he was a gaze, a presence, a vision of the world, a demand for excellence. AI can provide infinite availability, adaptation to pace, the capacity to follow any thread of thought, but it cannot provide meaning, perspective, framework, or even love, in the deepest sense of the word, the kind that consists in wanting the other person to become fully themselves. That is why the question is not to replace teachers with machines, which would be the worst of dystopias, but to free teachers from everything the machine can do, so that they can finally devote themselves to what it will never be able to do.

## 7. A day in the place

I now want to be concrete, because visions that remain abstract always end up dying at conferences, and because I want you to be able to see, almost to touch, this place I imagine. I no longer really call it a school, not quite, because the word is loaded with too many images, rows of desks, blackboards, bells. I call it a place, a place you come to in order to grow, and I want to take you inside with a ten-year-old child, let’s call him Léo, on a morning like any other.

Léo arrives. There is no bell, no lining up, no timetable pinned to the wall with subjects sliced into one-hour portions. There is a space, spaces rather, a library with real books, tables for working alone and others for working together, a workshop with tools, materials, screens too, but screens in the service of something, not screens to fill a void. And Léo doesn’t need to be told what he is going to do, because the moment he walks through the door he is caught up in what he loves, in what he is building, in his project. For two weeks now, Léo has wanted to understand why some birds migrate and others don’t, and he has set his heart on creating an animated map of the great migrations across his département.

So he sits down and picks up where he left off the day before. He talks with the AI, which helps him understand how migration works, which explains what a magnetic field is when he asks how birds find their way, which suggests he check his intuitions in a book from the library rather than handing him everything at once. And now he needs to measure distances, calculate speeds, understand proportions so that his map is accurate: mathematics arrives, not as a subject imposed at ten o’clock on a Tuesday, but as a tool he needs, right now, to see through what he wants to do. And when he wants to write the texts that will go with his map, French arrives in its turn, grammar, spelling, clarity of expression, because he wants others to understand him, because he wants to be read.

Around him, the guides move about. One of them stops beside Léo, looks at his map, and asks him a question he hadn’t asked himself: and humans, do they migrate too? And suddenly the project opens onto history, onto geography, onto population movements, onto the question of why people leave one place and why they choose another. She doesn’t give him a lesson, she opens a window for him, and it is he who will decide whether he wants to climb through that window today, tomorrow, or never. A little further on, another child, Inès, has already changed project three times this month; she needs to explore, to try her hand at everything, and nobody holds it against her, because everyone knows her path runs that way. Next to her, Malik has been working for two months on a single problem, a little robot that sorts rubbish; he takes it apart, puts it back together, analyses it from every angle, and nobody asks him to move on to something else, because everyone knows his path runs that way too.

And then there are the other moments, the ones that make a place of learning more than a collection of individuals each shut away in their own bubble with their machine. There is sport, the body, shared effort, play, victory and defeat lived together. There is community life, meals, looking after the place, collective decisions, the older ones helping the younger ones. There are shared projects for those who need others in order to create, and spaces of silence for those who need to concentrate alone. The collective does not disappear, it changes nature: it is no longer about being together because you are the same age and listening to the same lesson, it is about being together because you live, you play, you help each other, you build. And in the evening, when Léo gets home, he doesn’t say he had two hours of maths and one hour of history. He says he discovered that storks could read the sky. And he can’t wait to come back tomorrow.

## 8. From teacher to guide

In this place, the teaching profession does not disappear, it transforms, and I even believe it recovers its original nobility, the one it lost when it became a job of delivering a curriculum, managing a class, marking papers and filling in school reports. The teacher is no longer the one who stands in front of a class to deliver knowledge that everyone must receive in the same way at the same moment. That role, honestly, the machine already fills better than they do, because it has access to all the knowledge in the world and can rephrase it a thousand times without losing patience. The teacher becomes a guide, and that word is not a weakening, it is a deepening: they accompany people in their projects, help them follow their thinking through to the end, open up perspectives for them, pass on to them a vision of the world.

The heart of this new profession is the framework. Because this is not about leaving children alone with an AI and hoping everything will turn out fine; that would be abandonment disguised as freedom. A ten-year-old who is given a tool capable of doing everything is, of course, liable to ask it to do everything: to write his text, calculate his distances, draw his map. And that is precisely where the guide steps in, not to forbid, but to steer, to provide a framework, to point exploration in a fruitful direction, to show the child the difference between an AI that helps him think and an AI that thinks for him. No technical rule can draw that line once and for all; it is held case by case, in the relationship, in the gaze of an adult who knows the child and can recognise the moment when he needs help and the moment when he needs to be left to face the difficulty.

And then there is this new skill, perhaps the most important of all, that the guide must pass on: learning to converse with a machine the way one converses with a book, the way one converses with the world around us. A good reader doesn’t believe everything they read; they question the text, set it against others, look for the author’s intention, keep their freedom. A child learning to question an AI must learn the same thing: to ask precise questions, to doubt the answers, to check elsewhere, to rephrase, to contradict, to ask why, to look for what the machine doesn’t say. This is not a technical skill, it is a philosophical one, it is the Socratic art of questioning brought up to date. Socrates, whose mother was a midwife, said he practised the art of delivering minds: he did not pass on knowledge, he helped others discover what they already carried within them. Tomorrow’s guide is the heir to this maieutics, and AI is its instrument.

What delights me about this transformation is that it finally gives a sequel to the questions of the child I was. In the place I imagine, the child no longer learns answers, they learn to ask questions, and the singular question, the one that comes from far away and goes far, the one nobody expected, the question that made my class laugh because it threw them, becomes the very heart of learning. The guide no longer asks whether the question is on the curriculum; they ask where it comes from and where it might lead. And the AI, infinitely available, can follow the child down that path while the adult makes sure they don’t get lost along it.

## 9. The real fear

If all this is possible, and I sincerely believe all this is possible, technically, today, then we have to ask the uncomfortable question: why doesn’t it exist yet? Why, when we have tools that would make it possible to realise the dream of Montessori, of Freinet, of Illich, for every child, do we keep organising supervised tests to stop pupils from using them? The easy answer would be to say it is fear of AI, mistrust of technology, anxiety about screens. But I don’t believe that is the real reason, because we could have built this school long before AI, at least in part, and we didn’t.

The real fear runs deeper, and it is more human. It is the fear of changing what exists. The fear of evolution, the fear of testing, the fear of getting it wrong by trying something else. For there could have been, there could be as soon as tomorrow, test schools, pilot sites, courageous experiments where this model of personalised accompaniment would truly be tried, on a large scale, with resources, with honest evaluation. A few initiatives exist, carried by passionate teachers, committed parents, associations, but they remain fragile, isolated, often viewed with suspicion by the institution, as if every attempt to do things differently were an implicit criticism of what is already being done.

And perhaps that, deep down, is the real reason: a vision like this one does not merely improve school, it changes its very essence. It calls into question the single curriculum, the age group, the mark, the ranking, the timetable, the role of the teacher, and, more deeply still, our relationship to control. A school where every child follows their own path is a school that can no longer be steered from an office, a school where you can no longer easily compare, easily measure, easily standardise. It is a school that trusts the child, and trust, for a system built on standardisation, is an existential threat.

It has to be said kindly but bluntly: we are not protecting our children by refusing change, we are protecting ourselves. We are protecting our habits, our bearings, our institutions, our intellectual comfort. And meanwhile the tool is already here, in our children’s bedrooms, and they use it without us, without a framework, without guidance, often to do exactly what we dread, because nobody has taught them to do anything else. The real danger is not that AI enters school. The real danger is that it grows up alongside school, without it, and that school gradually becomes a place children pass through out of obligation while their real learning happens elsewhere, alone with machines nobody has taught them to question.

## 10. Learning to ask questions

I come back to that classroom, to that child who drifted off into his thoughts and came back with a question nobody understood. I picture him today, in the place I have tried to describe. He drifts off into his thoughts, and nobody calls him to order, because everyone knows he is pulling on a thread. He comes back with his question, and this time it doesn’t go nowhere. It becomes the starting point. The AI helps him dig into it, rephrase it, find the books and ideas that answer it or contradict it. A guide sits down beside him, listens, asks him where it came from, and opens up a perspective he had not imagined. And this child learns, perhaps for the first time, that his way of thinking is not a flaw to be corrected but a singularity to be cultivated.

The poet Rainer Maria Rilke, in his Letters to a Young Poet, advised loving the questions themselves, living them, rather than rushing towards answers one is not yet ready to receive. I believe this is what tomorrow’s school must teach: not answers, which the machine will always supply faster and better than we can, but the love of questions, the art of asking them, the courage to follow them, the clear-sightedness to doubt the answers we receive. In a world where answers are becoming infinite and free, value shifts towards the question. And a child who knows how to ask real questions will never be replaced by a machine, because the question is born of what is most deeply human in them: their desire, their wonder, their reaching towards what they do not yet know.

So I am speaking to you who teach, and who sense, deep down, that something no longer works, that your pupils are slipping away from you, that the curriculum is stifling you as much as them. I am speaking to you who are a parent, and who watch your child dim a little more each year in front of their homework, when they light up the moment they talk about what they love. I am speaking to you who think about education, who make decisions, who design policies, and to you who build artificial intelligences and wonder what they will really be for. What I am proposing is not a distant utopia, it is a concrete, immediate possibility that asks only one thing: the courage to try. Let’s open pilot sites. Let’s give them resources. Let’s allow teachers to become guides. Let’s allow children to follow their own paths. And let’s look honestly at what happens.

Artificial intelligence will not save education for us. It has no vision, no desire, no love. But it offers us what the pioneers who dreamed of a different school always lacked: time, availability, the possibility of accompanying each person according to who they are. What it offers us is the chance to finally give school back to every child. The rest, the vision, the framework, the trust, the attentive gaze, is ours to bring. More than two thousand years ago, Plutarch already understood that you do not educate a mind by filling it, but by kindling it. Today we hold in our hands a flame of unprecedented power. The only question that matters is this: will we go on using it to fill vessels, or will we finally dare to kindle fires?
