> *“It is not matter that thinks. It is the invisible taking on flesh.”*

## 1. A silent revolution

There are revolutions that make no noise, that have no barricades and no falling walls, no crowds in the streets and no footage to be replayed endlessly in documentaries, revolutions that begin with a barely perceptible tremor, a faint beat in the fabric of reality, a somewhat technical announcement lost between two geopolitical crises and three of the day’s controversies, and which we only understand years later had changed the frequency of the world. I had that feeling one morning in February 2025, reading that Microsoft was unveiling a new kind of quantum chip, named Majorana 1, a chip no bigger than the palm of a hand, which the company claimed opened a path toward machines that could, in time, line up a million qubits on a single component.

I am not a physicist, and I am not going to pretend to be one. But I have spent years designing digital products, I have watched technological waves roll in, I have learned to tell the announcements that are nothing but marketing from those that, behind the vocabulary of the press release, signal a real shift in what is possible. And that morning, what gripped me was not so much the chip itself as what it allowed one to glimpse: a world where machines would no longer simply calculate faster, but differently, a world where information could travel without ever being intercepted, a world where we might at last simulate nature in nature’s own language. I felt, very concretely, a kind of vertigo, the vertigo of those who sense a door opening a crack without yet knowing what lies behind it.

This text is an attempt to look through the gap in that door, and to do so honestly. For the quantum attracts fantasies the way light attracts moths: it is credited with everything, teleportation, telepathy, infinite energy, universal consciousness, and by crediting it with everything we end up understanding nothing of what it really is. I want to do something else. I want to start from the facts, from what researchers have actually demonstrated, from what they are actually building, and only then, saying so clearly, let the vision take flight. A vision that dares to project itself forward, but in which no physicist could find a lie. Because I believe that the reality of the quantum, when you look at it closely, is more beautiful than all the fantasies we paste over it.

And because I believe that the real question this revolution raises is not technical. It is human, and it is political: when we have machines capable of linking the entire planet in an inviolable network, who will control them, and what will we do with that bond?

## 2. The name of a vanished man

Before speaking of the chip, we must speak of the man whose name it bears, because his story is one of the strangest in twentieth-century science, and because it already says something about what is mysterious in the quantum. Ettore Majorana was born in 1906 in Catania, Sicily. In the 1930s, he was part of the small group of young physicists gathered around Enrico Fermi in Rome, the ones known as the boys of Via Panisperna, and Fermi himself, who was not a man to hand out compliments, is said to have placed him in the rarest category of scientists, that of geniuses like Galileo and Newton. Majorana published little, he was shy, solitary, demanding to the point of excess, he left sleeping in his drawers ideas that others would have proudly signed.

In 1937, however, he published a paper containing a dizzying idea: there might exist particles that are their own antiparticle, particles that are, in a certain way, both themselves and their own inverted reflection. These theoretical objects would later bear his name, Majorana fermions. And then, a few months later, in March 1938, Ettore Majorana vanished. He took a boat between Palermo and Naples, sent contradictory letters, one seeming to announce his death, the other retracting it, and was never seen again. Suicide, flight to a monastery, exile in South America, every hypothesis has been put forward, and decades later an Italian investigation even concluded that he had lived in Venezuela in the 1950s. The genius evaporated, leaving behind him a hypothetical particle and an enigma.

There is something deeply unsettling in the fact that, nearly a century later, it is precisely the idea of this vanished man that finds itself at the heart of a global technological race. For Microsoft’s approach rests on the search for particular quantum states, related to these Majorana fermions, that would make it possible to build so-called topological qubits, far more stable than the others, far less sensitive to the slightest noise from their environment, and therefore far easier to multiply. To get there, the company developed a new type of material, which it calls a topoconductor, a combination of indium arsenide and aluminum cooled to temperatures close to absolute zero.

But, and this is where honesty begins, it must also be said that this announcement aroused serious skepticism in the scientific community. The paper published in the journal Nature at the same time as the announcement came with an editorial note specifying that the results did not, in themselves, constitute proof of the presence of those famous Majorana states. And it was not the first time: a 2018 paper on the same subject, stemming from work linked to Microsoft, had to be retracted a few years later. In other words, the chip exists, the promise is immense, but the definitive proof that the vanished man’s idea is truly there, at the heart of the silicon, is still awaited. I find this almost poetic: even in the most advanced laboratories in the world, Ettore Majorana remains elusive.

## 3. Beyond binary

To understand why this research matters, we have to go back to what distinguishes a quantum computer from all those we have been using for seventy years. Our computers, from the smallest phone to the largest supercomputer, think in binary. Every piece of information is reduced to a sequence of bits, and every bit is either a zero or a one, never both, never in between. All the power of modern computing rests on this logic of exclusion, on the ability to string together, billions of times per second, simple choices between two mutually exclusive options. And this logic, I believe, has spilled far beyond the machines: it has seeped into the way we think, into our taste for clear-cut answers, stark oppositions, debates where you have to pick a side, true or false, for or against, us or them.

The quantum introduces something else. A qubit, the unit of information of a quantum computer, can exist in a superposition of states: it is neither simply zero nor simply one, it is a combination of both, in proportions that can vary infinitely, until the moment it is measured and tips into one or the other. And when several qubits are entangled, that is, bound to one another in a way that has no equivalent in our ordinary world, the state of the whole can no longer be described by describing each qubit separately. The whole is no longer the sum of its parts. The system exists as one indivisible whole.

We need to be precise about what this does and does not make possible. A quantum computer is not a classical computer that is faster at everything. For sending an email, watching a video or running a word processor, it would be of no use at all. But for certain categories of problems, those that require exploring a gigantic number of combinations, those where nature itself behaves in a quantum way, it could accomplish in a few hours what our best machines would take millennia to do. It is a specialized power, not a universal one, and that is precisely what makes it interesting: it does not replace our way of calculating, it opens up another.

And I cannot help seeing in this passage from bit to qubit an invitation that reaches beyond the laboratories. If nature, at its most fundamental level, does not work in either/or but in both/and, if it holds together possibilities that our ordinary logic would keep apart, then perhaps we too could learn to think differently. Not by giving up rigor, but by accepting that reality is more nuanced than our oppositions, that two truths can coexist until the moment we choose, that the world is not always divided into two camps. This is not physics, it is a metaphor, and I own it as such. But it is a metaphor that seems to me salutary, in an age when everything pushes toward polarization.

## 4. What the quantum will really change

In 1981, in a lecture that has become famous, the physicist Richard Feynman made a remark of disarming simplicity: nature is not classical, and if you want to simulate it, you have to do so with quantum mechanics. Our current computers struggle terribly to simulate the behavior of molecules, because the electrons they are made of obey quantum laws, and describing those laws with classical bits demands a computing power that explodes as soon as the molecule becomes even slightly complex. A quantum computer, by contrast, speaks the same language as matter. It could directly simulate what our machines can only roughly approximate.

That, to my mind, is where the most concrete and most underestimated revolution of the quantum lies. Imagine being able to design a drug by precisely simulating its interaction with a protein, instead of testing thousands of molecules blindly. Imagine being able to invent batteries that store far more energy, superconducting materials that carry electricity without loss, catalysts that turn carbon dioxide into fuel. These are not fantasies, they are precisely the applications researchers put forward, because they are the ones where the superiority of the quantum is most natural.

There is one example that fascinates me in particular. To feed humanity, we manufacture vast quantities of nitrogen fertilizer every year using an industrial process invented at the beginning of the twentieth century, which requires enormous temperatures and pressures, and which on its own consumes a significant share of the world’s energy, on the order of one or two percent depending on the estimates. Meanwhile, certain bacteria, in the roots of legumes, carry out the same transformation at room temperature, silently, without a factory, thanks to an enzyme whose workings we still do not fully understand, because its chemical core is too complex for our simulations. Understanding this enzyme is one of the goals regularly cited for future quantum computers. If we succeeded, we might be able to feed the world while spending a fraction of the energy we devote to it today.

This, I believe, is the true energy promise of the quantum, and it is more honest than the one we sometimes read about. The quantum will not produce free energy: quantum computers themselves consume energy, and a great deal of it, to keep themselves near absolute zero. But they could help us discover the materials, the processes and the molecules that change the cost of energy for all of humanity. Energy abundance will not come from a magic source. It will come from understanding. And perhaps it is more beautiful that way: it is not the machine that gives us energy, it is the knowledge of nature it allows us to acquire, so that we may finally learn to do as nature does, with less.

## 5. The quantum internet

In 2025, a team at the University of Oxford carried out an experiment that made a great deal of noise, and that was summed up, a little hastily, as the teleportation of information between two quantum computers. What the researchers actually did is both more modest and more important. They linked two small quantum processors, a few meters apart, with an optical fiber, and they used the entanglement between the two modules to execute a computing operation, a logic gate, that stretched across both machines, as if they formed only one. Rather than building a giant processor, ever harder to stabilize, they showed that smaller modules, linked by light, could be assembled to work together.

A few months later, in February 2026, Oxford announced that it would co-lead with the University of Tokyo a five-year project, funded by the United Kingdom and Japan, whose stated aim is to lay the foundations of a quantum internet: a network of quantum nodes linked by single photons, individual grains of light, capable of moving information in an ultra-secure way and of allowing distant machines to compute together. The project’s British lead, Professor David Lucas, summed it up in a simple formula: just as the internet connects classical computers, the future of quantum lies in networking quantum processors.

And this is where I have to be honest with myself, because for a long time I believed, like many others, that the quantum internet would be an instantaneous internet, with no latency, where information would cross the planet in a flash thanks to entanglement. That is not the case. When two particles are entangled, the measurement of one is indeed instantly correlated with that of the other, whatever the distance. But a fundamental result of physics, known as the no-communication theorem, shows that this correlation cannot be used to send a message faster than light. Every quantum teleportation, including Oxford’s, needs a classical channel to transmit part of the information, and that channel never travels faster than light. The quantum internet will not be faster than light.

But it could be something else, something deeper: inviolable. Thanks to what is called quantum key distribution, two people can share an encryption key in such a way that any attempt at interception leaves a detectable trace, because, in the quantum world, to observe is to disturb. This is not theory: as early as 2017, a Chinese satellite made it possible to establish a communication encrypted in this way between Beijing and Vienna. The quantum internet may be the first network in history where the spy can no longer listen without being seen. And it will also be a network where machines spread across the whole planet can pool their strength to solve together problems that none could solve alone. Not a faster world, then, but a more protected and more connected one. I find this truth more interesting than the fantasy.

## 6. One brain, or a thousand?

If we extend this trajectory, we see something taking shape that resembles a planetary brain: quantum machines and artificial intelligences linked together by a global network, capable of computing together, sharing knowledge, simulating nature, optimizing energy, designing drugs, on a scale beyond anything we have known. And this is where the vision can tip from dream to nightmare, depending on a single question: who controls this brain?

For a single planetary brain, centralized, owned by a handful of companies or states, would be the most formidable instrument of power ever built. Whoever held the greatest quantum computing power could, for example, break a large share of the encryption systems on which our banks, our messaging services and our public administrations rely today: the mathematician Peter Shor showed as early as 1994 that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer would be capable of it, and that is why the whole world is already working on new encryption systems able to withstand it. Whoever controlled the quantum network could decide who gets access to it, at what price, and for what purpose. The technology that could protect us from surveillance could also, if it is concentrated, hand a few people an unprecedented power.

My conviction is that this brain must not be single. There must be several, many, decentralized, locally controlled, linked to one another but independent of one another. Not a planetary brain, but a constellation of brains, like cities connected by roads rather than an empire governed from a capital. This idea is not utopian; on the contrary, it is written into the very history of the internet. In the 1960s, the engineer Paul Baran imagined a distributed network, with no center, able to survive even if some of its nodes were destroyed, and this headless architecture deeply inspired the first networks. The internet was designed to be decentralized. It was we, afterward, who let it become concentrated in the hands of a few giant platforms.

We have the chance not to make the same mistake again. Elinor Ostrom, winner of the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2009, showed that human communities know how to manage shared resources, forests, fisheries, irrigation networks, without handing them over either to a single owner or to a central authority, provided the rules are set locally by those who use them. All across Europe, citizens are already banding together in cooperatives to produce and share their own energy. Why not, tomorrow, computing cooperatives, quantum nodes that are municipal, regional, academic, community-run, linked to the global network but governed by those who depend on them? The more widely computing power is distributed, the fairer the sharing will be. Decentralization is not a technical detail: it is the condition for this revolution to liberate rather than enslave.

## 7. The end of surveillance?

We live in an age when surveillance has become the air we breathe. Our messages, our movements, our purchases, our searches, even our faces are collected, analyzed, resold, cross-referenced, by companies that trade in them and by states that turn them into an instrument of control. We have ended up finding this almost normal, as if privacy were a luxury from another time, a nostalgia for the days before screens. And part of our collective fatigue, I believe, comes from there: from that diffuse feeling of always being watched, measured, profiled, of never again being entirely alone with one’s thoughts.

A network where every interception leaves a trace could change that profoundly. It could give back to citizens the possibility of communicating without being overheard, to journalists the possibility of protecting their sources, to political dissidents the possibility of organizing without fear of the secret police, to patients the guarantee that their medical data will not be read by others. The Oxford and Tokyo project in fact plans to test its architecture with an artificial intelligence technique that makes it possible to train models on scattered data without ever gathering or exposing it. Here we glimpse a world where collective intelligence would no longer demand the sacrifice of each person’s privacy, where we could learn together without handing everything over.

But we must look at the other side with the same clarity. An inviolable network protects everyone, including those who should not be protected: it could armor the communications of authoritarian regimes as much as those of their opponents, those of criminals as much as those of journalists. And above all, the inviolability of the channel says nothing about those who control its endpoints. If the quantum network is centralized, if a few players own the nodes, inviolability will protect them, not us. So it is not technology that will put an end to surveillance. It is the way we govern it. An inviolable and decentralized network could be the most powerful tool of freedom in history. An inviolable and centralized network could be the most impregnable fortress of power.

## 8. Entanglement as a mirror

I now want to leave the ground of facts for a moment, saying so clearly, to let the vision speak. For there is in quantum physics an image that has lived in me ever since I became interested in it, and that seems to me to say something profound about what we are. Two particles that have interacted can remain entangled, whatever the distance that later separates them. You can move them to the two ends of the universe, they go on forming a single system, and what happens to one is correlated with what happens to the other. Einstein himself found this idea so disturbing that he spoke of spooky action at a distance. Decades of experiments have nonetheless confirmed that nature does indeed work this way.

I am not claiming that human beings are entangled in the physical sense of the term. That would be false, and I promised myself not to lie. But I cannot help seeing in this property of matter a mirror of something many of us feel without being able to prove it: that we are connected, that encounters leave traces that do not fade with distance, that what happens to a human being on the other side of the world concerns us in a way that our separating logic does not know how to name. Spiritual traditions have spoken for millennia of unity, of interdependence, of communion. The quantum does not prove them right. But it shows that separation, even at the most fundamental level of matter, is not always the rule.

In the middle of the twentieth century, the Jesuit and paleontologist Pierre Teilhard de Chardin imagined that the evolution of the Earth would lead to the emergence of a noosphere, a layer of thought enveloping the planet the way the atmosphere envelops it, made of all human consciousnesses linked together. Many have seen in the internet a first step toward that vision. I believe we have built only its caricature: a network that links machines but often separates people, that circulates information but also anger, that connects without bringing together. Perhaps the next generation of networks, more protected, more distributed, more respectful of each person, could bring us a little closer to what Teilhard sensed: not a single brain that would absorb us, but a communion of free consciousnesses.

That is the vision I carry, and I carry it as a vision, not as a prediction. Technology will not make us telepathic. But it could remind us, through what it teaches us about matter, that we are not islands. And that reminder, in a fractured age, is not a luxury.

## 9. Are we ready?

Everything I have just described is possible. Nothing is guaranteed. And the real question is not whether engineers will manage to stabilize qubits, to build ever more extensive quantum networks, to make these machines useful. They will probably manage it, sooner or later, because billions are being invested and the best minds on the planet are working on it. The real question is at once inward and political: will we know how to welcome this power without reproducing our old patterns of control, domination and concentration?

Every great technological revolution has carried the same promise of liberation and has often led to new forms of power. The printing press was supposed to free knowledge; it also served propaganda. Radio was supposed to bring peoples together; it also carried the speeches of dictators. The internet was supposed to decentralize power and give everyone a voice; it also gave rise to the greatest concentrations of wealth and influence in history. It is not technology that decides what it becomes. It is the consciousness of those who design it, of those who use it, and of those who govern it.

So we have to ask the questions before it is too late, while the architectures are still taking shape, while the standards are being written, while investments are choosing their directions. Who will own the nodes of the future quantum network? Will universities, local governments and citizens have their place, or only the giants? Will the discoveries made possible by quantum simulation, drugs, materials, energy processes, be treated as common goods of humanity or as patents to be made profitable? Will computing resources be shared fairly between the North and the South? These questions are not technical. They are democratic, and they concern us all.

There is also a more intimate question. If tomorrow we have a network that lets us communicate without being watched, compute together without handing everything over, understand nature as never before, what will we do with it? Will we go on using our tools to compare ourselves, to quarrel, to distract ourselves, or will we learn to use them to understand one another, to cooperate, to create together? No chip, however revolutionary, will answer that question for us.

## 10. A threshold

Majorana 1 is not an end. Nor is it a magic promise, and those who present it as such are either mistaken or misleading us. The full scientific proof has yet to be delivered, useful machines have yet to be built, the global quantum network has yet to be invented, and all of this will take years, perhaps decades. But something has been set in motion, in the laboratories of Microsoft, Oxford, Tokyo and many other places, something that looks like a threshold. And on the other side of that threshold lies a world that will not look quite like ours.

That world will probably not be faster, in the sense we mean today: light will remain the limit, and that is just as well. But it could be more intelligent, in the sense that we would at last understand nature finely enough to learn to do as it does, with less energy, less waste, less violence done to the Earth. It could be more protected, in the sense that everyone would regain the right to speak without being overheard. It could be more connected, in the sense that machines and beings spread across the whole planet would learn to cooperate without dissolving into a single center. On one condition, always the same: that it be decentralized, shared, governed by those who depend on it.

I think again of Ettore Majorana, boarding that boat between Palermo and Naples in 1938, and disappearing into the night with ideas too big for his time. He left behind him a particle that is its own reflection, an enigma we have still not solved, and an intuition that, nearly a century later, could change the way humanity computes, communicates and connects. There is in this story a lesson in humility: the most powerful ideas often come from those who doubt, who withdraw, who refuse to put themselves forward. And a lesson in hope: an intuition can pass through silence, disappearance and time, and in the end take on flesh.

The quantum is not a god, and Microsoft’s chip is not an oracle. But they may be a mirror, an invitation to rewrite our relationship with knowledge, with matter, with energy and with one another. What awaits us on the other side of the threshold is not a faster world. It may be a freer world, if we have the wisdom to build it together rather than let it be built by a few.

> *“A time will come when we no longer speak of progress, but of harmony.”*
