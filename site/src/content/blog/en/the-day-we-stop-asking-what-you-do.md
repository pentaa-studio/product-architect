> “I think it’s actually going to be universal high income. Where anyone can have any goods or services that they want.”  
> — Elon Musk

## 1. The promise

It is sometimes hard to tell whether Elon Musk is playing the oracle or the provocateur, and no doubt he does not always know himself, but there is something in this sentence, tossed off in the middle of an interview between two nods to science fiction, that deserves we stop, that we take it seriously, that we turn it over in every direction, because it says out loud what many of those building artificial intelligence quietly think: that these machines, if they keep their promises, will produce an abundance so great that the question of work, of subsistence, of the wage we exchange for our time, could cease to be the central question of our lives. Musk is not talking about a universal income in the usual sense, that minimal safety net stretched beneath the most fragile so they do not fall too far, he is talking about a universal high income, about a world where everyone could have access to all the goods and all the services they desire, about a flood rather than a net.

One can smile at this prophecy, one can find it naive or self-serving, coming from a man who owns a good share of the machines whose miracles he announces, and I will come back to this point later, because it is essential. But one can also try the exercise of taking him at his word, of saying to oneself: let’s grant it, let’s grant that robots produce, that AI organizes, that productivity reaches levels that make absurd the very idea that we must work forty hours a week to house ourselves, feed ourselves, care for ourselves. Let’s grant that it happens, not in a century, but in one or two decades, in our lifetime, in our children’s lifetime. Then a question arises immediately, a dizzying question, and it is this question that occupies me in this text, far more than the technical feasibility of the prophecy: what becomes of the human being who no longer needs to earn a living?

For we have built our existences, our identities, our prides and our shames, our days and our calendars, our cities and our transport, our schools and our pensions, around that very necessity. Work is not only what we do to live, it has become the grid through which we look at one another, the measure of our worth, the answer we give when we are asked who we are. Removing that necessity is not simply freeing up time, it is removing the spine of an entire civilization, and the question is not whether we will remain standing without it, but what might grow in its place.

I believe, and this is the conviction that runs through these pages, that what can grow in its place is more beautiful than what we had, on one condition: that we change the way we see, the way we see others, ourselves, and wealth itself. Material abundance is not an end. It is a beginning. And everything depends on what we make of that beginning.

## 2. “So, what do you do?”

There is a question we ask dozens of times a year, almost without thinking, at every dinner where we meet someone new, at every party, at every wedding, every time we find ourselves next to a stranger and have to say something, and that question is: “So, what do you do?” It seems harmless, polite, almost kind, a way of opening the conversation, of finding common ground. And yet, if we truly listen to it, it says something rather terrible about the world we live in. It does not ask what you love, or what makes you feel alive, or how you are, or what you have discovered lately. It asks for your function. It asks for your place in the productive machine. It asks, in short, what you are good for.

And the answer we give to this question determines, often within a few seconds, the way the other person will look at us for the rest of the evening. Doctor, entrepreneur, engineer: they sit up a little, they take an interest. Cashier, unemployed, temp worker: they nod, they politely look for another topic. It is no one’s fault in particular, it is the prism through which our society has learned to see, a prism that reduces the person to their economic usefulness, that measures the worth of a life by the market value of what it produces. And we have internalized this prism so thoroughly that we apply it to ourselves, that we judge ourselves through it, that we feel ashamed when our answer is not impressive enough, and that we feel lost the day we no longer have any answer at all, out of work, in retirement, in illness.

The anthropologist David Graeber showed how many of our modern jobs are experienced by those who hold them as devoid of meaning, jobs whose holders themselves believe could disappear without the world being any worse off, and which they nevertheless go on doing, because one has to earn a living, because one has to have an answer to the dinner-party question. There is in this situation a form of silent, massive suffering that we have come to find normal: spending most of our waking hours doing something that is not like us, so that we can say we do something. The stress that follows is not only that of the workload, it is that of being constantly evaluated, measured, compared, through a grid that says almost nothing about who we really are.

So let us imagine, just for a moment, a world where this question would no longer be the first one asked. A world where earning a living would no longer be the condition for having the right to live, and where, as a result, economic function would no longer be the first criterion by which we look at someone. That world is the one abundance could make possible. But before we step into it, we must remember that we have dreamed it before, and that we have missed it before.

## 3. An old dream

In 1930, in the depths of the Great Depression, while the whole world seemed to be collapsing into poverty and mass unemployment, the economist John Maynard Keynes published a short and astonishing essay, almost insolent in the context of the time, titled *Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren*. In it he asserted that within a century, thanks to technical progress and the accumulation of capital, the standard of living in rich countries would be several times higher than in his own day, and that the economic problem, the problem of subsistence, would be essentially solved. His grandchildren, he imagined, might work around fifteen hours a week, and that would be quite enough. But Keynes immediately added something deeper, and more troubled: he warned that the real challenge would then no longer be economic but existential, that humanity would find itself facing its real and permanent problem, that of knowing how to use its freedom, how to fill this liberated time, how to live well, wisely, and agreeably.

Keynes was not the first to dream this way. Fifty years earlier, Paul Lafargue, Marx’s son-in-law, had published a joyful and ferocious pamphlet, *The Right to Be Lazy*, in which he attacked what he called the strange madness of the love of work, and imagined machines that would free people so they could at last enjoy life. And two years after Keynes, the philosopher Bertrand Russell wrote his *In Praise of Idleness*, in which he defended the idea that a four-hour working day would be more than enough to produce everything we need, and that the time thus freed would allow everyone to cultivate what truly gives a life its worth: curiosity, art, science, conversation, play.

Nearly a century later, we have to face the facts: these thinkers were right about what was possible, and wrong about the path. Productivity did explode, beyond even what Keynes imagined, our societies are incomparably richer than in 1930, and yet we still work just as much, sometimes more, and above all with more stress, more intensity, more constant connection, more fear of losing what we have. The wealth produced by machines did not turn into free time for all, it turned into consumption for some, into profits for others, and into a permanent race for most. The dream was betrayed not by technology, but by the way we chose to distribute its fruits.

That is why I do not believe that AI, on its own, will fulfill Musk’s prophecy, any more than the steam engine or the computer fulfilled Keynes’s. Technology creates the possibility. What we do with that possibility depends on something else: on the way we see, on our collective choices, on our courage. And before speaking of those choices, I want to speak of something much simpler, much more intimate: what I, myself, would do with that freedom.

## 4. What I would do

I asked myself the question honestly, without trying to shine, without picturing myself as a liberated genius who would at last write his magnum opus or found a new civilization, and the answer that came to me was of a simplicity that at first surprised me a little, then moved me deeply. If tomorrow I never again needed to earn a living, I would first of all be less stressed, and I believe everything else flows from that. I would take the time to write, really write, without asking myself whether it serves any purpose. I would tinker, I would repair, I would build things with my hands. I would spend time with my family, with my friends, without looking at the clock, without those moments being parentheses stolen from an overfull week. I would surely plant a vegetable garden in the summer. I would join more community groups, I would go and give my time where it counts.

It is not spectacular. It is not the kind of program you present at a conference on the future of humanity. And yet, the more I think about it, the more I believe that this is precisely where the truth of abundance lies: it will not lead us first toward more things, it will bring us back toward more bonds, more time, more earth. The first face of a world freed from compulsory work is not rockets and futuristic cities, it is people taking the time to have lunch together, children who see their parents in the afternoon, neighbors who know one another, community groups that no longer lack volunteers, hands in the clay, hands in the soil.

There is something deeply symbolic in the vegetable garden, and I did not choose it by chance. Planting a seed means accepting a time that is not the time of productivity, a slow, cyclical time that refuses to be hurried, that asks for attention, patience, presence. It means finding again a bond with something larger than oneself, the season, the rain, the soil, what some traditions call rootedness, what others associate with the root chakra, that base which connects us to the Earth and gives us the security of existing. We have lived for generations above ground, accelerated, uprooted, and perhaps the first thing we will do with our freedom will be to replant ourselves.

And I do not believe I am an exception. I believe that if we asked most people the question, sincerely, far from injunctions and images of success, many would answer something very close: time for the people we love, for making things with our hands, for learning, for helping, for creating a little, for resting too. Abundance will not make us extraordinary. It may make us, at last, ordinary in the noblest sense of the word, that is to say fully human.

## 5. The golden abyss is not inevitable

People will object, and the objection is serious, that not everyone would plant a vegetable garden. That many, freed from work, would sink into their couches, in front of screens, into endless series, into networks that capture their attention, into a compulsive consumption that would fill the void left by the absence of constraint. I myself wrote, in a first version of this text, that abundance without an inner awakening could become a trap, a golden abyss, a world saturated with content, with services, with distractions, and with emptiness. That risk exists, and I do not want to brush it aside.

But on further reflection, I believe we are often mistaken about the cause of that emptiness. We think that overconsumption and retreat into screens are human nature left to itself, what people would naturally do if they were not forced to work. I believe it is the opposite. I believe these behaviors are compensations, symptoms of a life that is stressed, judged, exhausted, a life in which we spend our days doing something that is not like us and in which, in the evening, we look for a small dose of pleasure, of reward, of oblivion, in buying or in endless scrolling. We consume because we have been consumed. We shut ourselves in at home because the outside world has worn us out. We distract ourselves because we no longer have the energy to meet one another.

When we are no longer stressed by the necessity of earning a living, when it is no longer through that prism that we are judged, when it is no longer through that lens that people look at us, then we begin to be seen for something else: for who we are. And that change in the way we are seen changes everything. There is more consideration, and consideration is perhaps the deepest and most neglected human need of our time. People who feel considered are better off. And people who are better off have less need to compensate. They have less need to overconsume, less need to hide behind a screen, less need to fill a void, because the void fills up in another way, through bonds, through projects, through the simple fact of existing in the eyes of others.

I am not naive: there will always be people who shut themselves away, who lose themselves, who consume without end, because that too is what it means to be human, with our fragilities, our wounds, our addictions. But I deeply believe that the trend will reverse, that these behaviors will decrease more than they increase, because their root, stress and constant judgment, will be partly torn out. The golden abyss is not a fate written into abundance. It is the product of a way of seeing. And a way of seeing can be changed.

## 6. The new questions

So let us return to that dinner, to that encounter, to that moment when we find ourselves facing a stranger and have to say something. In the world I imagine, no one will ask “So, what do you do?” anymore, or rather, the question will change its meaning, because what we do will no longer be reduced to what we sell. We will ask other questions. Questions like: “Are you happy?” Like: “What’s lighting you up these days?” Like: “What do you love?” Like: “How are you, really?” Like: “Have you written anything new? Have you created anything new?”

These questions, if we listen to them closely, sketch out an entire civilization on their own. “So, what do you do?” measures a function. “What’s lighting you up these days?” meets a person. The first question ranks, the second connects. The first asks where you stand in the hierarchy, the second asks where you are on your path. The first has a fixed answer, a job you wear like a label for years, the second has a living answer, one that changes with the seasons of life, that might be a vegetable garden this summer, a book this winter, a community group next year, a child you are helping along, a guitar you are learning, a house you are restoring.

There is in this simple shift of vocabulary a revolution deeper than all economic reforms. For the questions we ask shape the answers we try to give, and the answers we try to give shape the life we lead. A society that asks each person what they produce manufactures anxious producers. A society that asks each person what lights them up brings forth living beings. And I believe this is the true stake of abundance: moving from a civilization of function to a civilization of momentum.

Imagine for a moment what this would change for a teenager wondering what to do with their life. Instead of asking which job will pay enough, which track offers the most openings, which box they should aim for to be respectable, they could simply ask what makes them come alive, what they want to explore, what they want to give. Imagine what it would change for a sixty-year-old who retires and suddenly feels useless, because they no longer have an answer to the dinner-party question: they would already have had, for a long time, another way of introducing themselves, another way of existing in the eyes of others.

## 7. Creation as nature

If we ask these new questions, we quickly discover that the answer, for most people, has to do with creation, in the broadest and humblest sense of the word. Creating is not only painting canvases or composing symphonies, it is writing a letter, growing tomatoes, repairing a chair, inventing a recipe, organizing a neighborhood party, teaching your child a song, starting a community group, building a project with friends. We are born of creation, and we are ourselves creators, each in our own way, and it is not a talent reserved for an elite, it is not an artist’s hobby, it is a deep nature, a spark each of us carries, the power to imagine something that does not yet exist, and then to bring it into existence.

Watch a child playing with two pebbles and a piece of wood: they invent an entire world, with its rules, its characters, its dramas, and no one taught them how. We all had this capacity, and most of us have let it fall asleep, not because it disappeared, but because adult life, organized around compulsory work, no longer left it any room. There was no more time, no more energy, no more legitimacy. Creating for nothing, without it paying, without it serving any purpose, has become almost suspect, a waste of time, a luxury we allow ourselves on Sundays if we are not too tired.

In a world of abundance, this nature can awaken. And it can awaken in a new way, freed from the obligation to monetize. For the trap would be to recreate, in this liberated world, a capitalism of creation, where every work would have to be rated, sold, promoted, where likes would replace sincere looks and where we would create for the algorithm rather than for ourselves and for others. Jeremy Rifkin described this possible shift toward a sharing society, in which a growing share of what we produce could circulate almost for free, without passing through the market. When we no longer need to sell what we make, we rediscover the pure pleasure of creating for the sake of creating, of sharing because it overflows, of giving because it is beautiful.

Creation then becomes an act of relationship rather than a product. We no longer ask “what’s it worth?”, we ask “what did it do to me?”. The tomato from the garden shared with the neighbor, the text read aloud to friends, the chair repaired for a community group, the song learned together: none of this appears in any growth statistic, and yet this may be where true wealth lies, the kind that nourishes, the kind that connects, the kind that gives the feeling of being alive.

## 8. Abundance for whom?

And now we have to talk about the uncomfortable part, about what Musk’s prophecy forgets to say, or says too quickly, about what could turn the most beautiful of dreams into a nightmare. For abundance does not fall from the sky. It is produced by machines, artificial intelligences, robots, data centers, and these machines today belong to a handful of companies and a handful of men among the richest in the history of humankind, Musk himself included. If these machines produce the wealth, and if those who own them keep most of it, then abundance will not be universal. It will be confiscated. It will create a world in which a minority owns everything the machines produce, and in which the majority, deprived of work and therefore of income, depends on the goodwill of that minority to survive.

This is, without any doubt, the greatest risk of the era now opening, and we must look it in the face, with clarity. What does abundance mean? Where does wealth come from? To whom does it belong? How do we share it? These questions are not technical, they are political, and perhaps even deeper still: they touch the paradigm in which we all live, all over the planet, that deeply rooted idea that whoever owns the tool also owns what the tool produces. As long as this paradigm remains intact, AI will not free humanity, it will concentrate wealth as no technology before it ever has. For abundance to return to everyone, it is not enough to change technology, we must change the way we see, and see the wealth produced by machines for what it really is: the fruit of centuries of accumulated knowledge, of data produced by each and every one of us, of infrastructure paid for by all, and therefore a good that should, at least in part, return to all.

There are already examples showing that it is possible. Norway made a remarkable choice when it discovered oil in its waters: rather than letting that wealth be captured by a few, it decided that it belonged to all Norwegians, and to their descendants. It placed it in a sovereign wealth fund, which has become one of the largest in the world, managed for future generations, and which today represents the equivalent of several hundred thousand dollars per inhabitant. In Alaska, since the early 1980s, every resident receives each year a dividend drawn from the state’s oil revenues. And as early as the late eighteenth century, Thomas Paine was already proposing that every citizen receive a share of the value of the land, on the grounds that the land, originally, belongs to everyone.

Why not imagine the same thing for artificial intelligence? A common fund, fed by a share of the wealth produced by machines, and redistributed to each person, not as charity, but as a dividend, as the share that is rightfully theirs of a common inheritance. Elinor Ostrom, the first woman to receive the Nobel Prize in economics, spent her life showing that human communities know how to manage common goods, fisheries, forests, irrigation systems, without handing them over either to the market or to the state alone. AI could become the greatest commons in history, or the greatest private property in history. That choice is not technical. It is in our hands, and it will be made, one way or another, in the years to come.

## 9. The role of AI

In all this, what place is there for artificial intelligence itself, beyond its function as a production machine? I believe it can be two very different things, and that here again, everything depends on what we make of it. It can be a tool of alienation, a machine that decides for us, that captures our attention, that locks us inside bubbles of content tailored to keep us hooked, that optimizes our consumption, that makes us dependent and passive. Or it can be a lever of liberation, a machine that relieves us of what exhausts us, that gives us back time, and that, perhaps, helps us ask ourselves the right questions.

For a well-designed AI should not tell us what to do with our lives. It should help us understand what lights us up. It could be, for someone who wants to write, a patient conversation partner who helps them follow their thought all the way through. For someone who wants to plant a vegetable garden, a source of knowledge about soils, seasons, companion plants. For someone who wants to tinker, a companion who helps them understand how to repair what they thought was lost. For someone who wants to get involved, a tool that helps them find where their time would be most useful. It can offer answers, but above all, it can ask questions, the very ones we should be asking one another: what makes you come alive, what do you truly want, and what if you allowed yourself to believe in it?

There is an important reversal here. AI is often presented as a threat to humans, a competitor that will replace us, make us useless. And it will be, indeed, if we keep measuring human worth by economic usefulness, since on that ground the machine will end up winning almost everywhere. But if we change the measure, if we stop asking humans to be useful and start asking them to be alive, then the machine ceases to be a competitor. It becomes what it should always have been: a tool in the service of life, taking care of what is mechanical so that we can devote ourselves to what is human.

This reversal will not happen on its own. It requires that those who design these machines ask themselves what they are designing them for, that those who use them learn to use them without losing themselves in them, and that our societies decide collectively what place they want to give them. But it is possible, and it is perhaps the only path worth taking.

## 10. What will you dare to live?

I come back to Musk’s sentence, to that promise of a world where everyone could have all the goods and all the services they desire. I now believe that it frames the question badly, or rather that it stops at the threshold of the real question. For the goal was never to have everything we desire. The goal is to live a life that has meaning, a life in which we are seen for who we are, in which we have time to love, to create, to pass things on, to connect with others and with the Earth. Material abundance is only the condition of that life. It is not its content. The content is ours to write.

We may be living through a bifurcation unique in human history. On one side, a world where machines produce everything, where wealth is concentrated in a few hands, where the majority, deprived of work and of being seen, sinks into compensation and emptiness. On the other, a world where machines free up time for all, where wealth is shared as a common good, where we ask one another what lights us up rather than what we produce, and where each person can at last cultivate what they carry within, their vegetable garden, their friendships, their writings, their commitments, their share of creation. Both worlds are possible. Neither is written in advance. And the difference between the two does not lie in the power of the machines, but in the depth of the way we see.

I once wrote that we were perhaps all tired gods, and that true freedom consisted in remembering. I still believe it. We are tired from centuries of compulsory work, of survival, of judgment, of racing. And abundance, if we know how to share it, could be the moment when we finally rest, when we breathe, and when we remember what we are: beings made to create, to love, to connect, to make things grow.

So the day may come when, at a dinner, someone will turn to you, and instead of asking what you do, will simply ask you: “What’s lighting you up these days?” On that day, you will need to have an answer. Not a brilliant answer, not an impressive answer, just a true answer. And it is perhaps now, starting today, even before abundance arrives, that we must begin looking for it. For the question is not what AI is going to do for you. The question is: what will you dare to live, now that you could be free?
